# Interview practice coaching rubric

Version: 2026-09-28.3

This is the project’s practice coaching rubric. It is not an official TCLA marking scheme. Both analysis prompts use this document, applying only the rules that their inputs can support. The original question bank supplies the exact question and category.

## Shared practice principles

Review what happened in this answer and suggest a small number of concrete changes for another attempt. Keep the question and available evidence central. Prepare ideas and relevant examples rather than requiring a memorised script or one universal answer formula. Be direct about missing relevance; a short or off-topic answer does not require praise. Do not invent a candidate's experience or a fact to fill a gap. Suggested templates must use clearly labelled placeholders.

The written advice recommends practising alone on camera, replaying the answer and choosing one observable adjustment. Its references to genuineness, enthusiasm and team connection describe human reviewer impressions. They do not justify automated personality, emotion, honesty, motivation or team-fit measurements.

## Content review: transcript and exact question only

Use a 0–10 practice score for each assessable criterion: 0–2 means missing or off-topic, 3–4 means weak with material gaps, 5–6 means developing with relevant but incomplete support, 7–8 means good with clear relevant explanation, and 9–10 means strong and well-supported. Use null with `not_assessable` where evidence is insufficient. These scores grade the answer, not the person's character or employability. Explain what worked, what did not, why and how to improve. If there is no clear strength, say that plainly instead of inventing praise. The server averages assessable criterion scores for an answer, and averages the three content-area scores for a session; camera presentation remains separate in the session grade.

1. **Answer the exact prompt.** Relevant experiences or skills are not automatically an explanation of the reason the question asks for. Explain the missing link directly.
2. **Explain specific context and significance.** An event, task, observation or conversation can support an answer if the speaker explains why it mattered. An open day can be as useful as formal work experience. Do not demand a prestigious experience.
3. **For commercial-law motivation, connect the experience to the stated interest.** Distinguish a CV summary from an explanation of what appealed to the candidate. Check how exploration developed the interest, what later experiences confirmed or changed, and what contribution the speaker wants to make. Assess the reasons expressed; do not claim to establish inner motivation or authenticity.
4. **Carry the personal thread through the answer.** A memorable opening needs relevant development. Clarify missing context, such as what kind of practice a family member established. Avoid generic jargon that replaces an explanation. A law-versus-banking comparison is useful only when it explains this speaker's actual reasoning.
5. **Use the appropriate category.** Competency answers need relevant context, the speaker's actions, the outcome and any useful reflection. A structure such as STAR is optional. Firm questions need firm relevance rather than a generic commercial-law story. Business, ethical and technical prompts need reasoned consideration of the supplied facts; missing case materials and unverified current claims remain limitations.
6. **Keep structure and spoken clarity proportionate.** Check linked ideas and a clear conclusion. Do not treat a stumble or rehearsal as proof of a weak answer, reading a script or dishonesty. Teamwork and willingness to lead are compatible preferences; they are not a contradiction by themselves.

Evidence selects a provided segment ID. The server attaches that segment's exact transcript text and start time. Do not generate quotations or guessed timestamps. Transcription can be wrong, so invite replay where needed. Suspected transcription mistakes (for example a job title misheard as another word) must not lower any score or become advice to correct pronunciation or terminology. State the uncertainty and ask for replay instead. Do not demand the elimination of every filler word; focus on repetition that prevents the meaning from being followed. Examples may come from study, societies, volunteering, personal projects or paid work; never require prior legal or professional employment. The content model has no audio or images: it cannot assess vocal energy, accent, volume, facial expression or eye contact. WPM and pause counts are local ASR estimates, not scores for confidence or fluency.

## Camera review: sampled stills only

Apply the written advice's framing, comfortable upright posture and review-of-visible-habits guidance only where the sampled images support it. Describe visible head direction, apparent eye direction, mouth or facial positioning, visible hands/arms, posture, framing, lighting or background. Distinguish a directly visible head turned away from an unsupported claim of distraction, reading, dishonesty or lack of interest. Do not interpret facial expression as an emotion or personality trait.

The source suggests smiling and bringing energy as optional presentation tips. It explicitly treats these as suggestions, not rigid requirements or proxies for motivation. Do not demand a smile, continually open eyes or uninterrupted eye contact. Blinking and brief natural glances are normal. When useful, suggest returning toward the lens while allowing natural glances, or adjusting the camera so the face and any relevant gesture remain comfortably visible.

Choose at most four distinct useful observations. Cite a supplied frame ID; the server derives the timestamp of that observed sample. A still can show positioning at a moment, but cannot establish exact onset, duration or continuous movement. Avoid repeating one issue at many sample times. If a feature is unclear or outside the frame, state the limit rather than inventing a gesture.

Each observation needs a concise practical suggestion and a brief explanation of why it matters for visibility or ease of communication. A comfortable frame can make an explanation easier to follow; it does not prove competence or predict a hiring result. Camera feedback cannot assess the spoken answer or vocal delivery. Empty observations are valid when nothing useful is supported.
